# General rubric for student and early-career CVs (international market, English)

Based on guidance from university career offices (Harvard, MIT, Columbia, Berkeley, Penn, Oxford, LSE),
recruiters (McKinsey, BCG, Bain, Google) and practitioner guides. See docs/sources.md.

## Criteria (score each 1-5)
1. **Target fit**: written for this role and company type. Most relevant items first; irrelevant items cut.
   The CV mirrors the vacancy's language and keywords where true (Google recruiters, MIT, Oxford).
2. **Impact bullets**: results, not duties. Formula: action verb + what + how + result
   (Columbia; Google's "Accomplished X as measured by Y by doing Z"). Weak openers when no result follows:
   "Responsible for", "Helped", "Assisted", "Worked on", "Participated in", "Supported".
3. **Evidence and numbers**: scale and results are quantified (people, money, rows, users, %, time saved,
   placement out of N teams). Tools and methods appear inside bullets, not only in a skills list (MIT).
   Self-descriptions ("problem solver", "team player") are shown, never claimed (Bain).
4. **Structure and scannability**: education first for students; reverse chronological order; consistent
   formatting; dates as "Month Year"; right-aligned dates; standard section names.
5. **Length and density**: one page for students and recent graduates (MIT, Penn, Columbia, Google for
   business roles). Two pages only for UK/European research or legal roles when every line is relevant.
   2-5 bullets per entry, 1-2 lines each. Margins at least 0.5", font 10pt or larger.
6. **ATS-friendliness**: single column, no text boxes, icons, photos, graphics or tables for layout; no key
   info in headers or footers; standard fonts; PDF with selectable text; spell out abbreviations once
   (e.g. "Amazon Web Services (AWS)").
7. **Language quality**: zero typos (two spelling errors measurably cut interview chances, NN/g citing
   Sterkens et al.); no personal pronouns; active voice; consistent tense (past for past roles).

## Section order for students
Header (name; city and country; email; phone; LinkedIn; GitHub/portfolio when relevant) ->
Education (university, program, expected graduation, GPA with scale; at most one line of
target-relevant coursework, honors, thesis, exchange or scholarship) -> Experience -> Projects -> Leadership & Activities ->
Skills (grouped) -> Languages (CEFR level or test score) -> optional Awards / Interests.
Move Projects above Experience when projects are more relevant. No "Objective" paragraph;
a 1-2 line summary only if it is specific.

## Grades from an unfamiliar system
State the scale and, where possible, a rank: "GPA 91/100 (top 10% of cohort)", "GPA 3.8/4.0".
National exam results with a percentile, not a raw score (McKinsey: put results in context).
A missing GPA is often read as a low one (NN/g).

## Leave out (common in Ukrainian and Europass-style CVs)
Photo, date of birth or age, marital status, nationality, gender, full street address, the title
"Curriculum Vitae", references or "references available on request", soft-skill lists without evidence,
casual hobbies. City and country are enough (LSE, Harvard, MIT, UK National Careers Service).
School achievements only if exceptional (olympiad medal) and the candidate is in years 1-2.

## Signal vs noise (check every line)
Space on one page is the scarcest resource. A line earns its place only if a recruiter for this target
would value it. Flag for cutting or shortening:
- Details of how a requirement was met rather than what was achieved: passing a course by external
  exam, credit transfers, exemptions, "granted advanced credit". To a recruiter this reads as showing off
  and says little about job performance. Keep only if the target is academic or quantitative research
  and the fact is rare, and then at most half a line.
  Not the same as a rank or percentile against other people ("Ranked 1st of admitted students",
  "national exam top 3%", "2nd of 65 teams"): those compare the candidate with peers and are strong signal.
- Generic interests ("Gym", "Travel"), unless they start a conversation relevant to the role. An interest
  with a distinctive achievement ("Speedcubing, national champion") is worth keeping: it is memorable.
- Coursework lists that are long, generic or unrelated to the target. Keep 2-4 courses that map directly
  to the role, or none.
- Bullets that describe content or topics instead of the candidate's contribution
  ("Recent episodes cover X, Y, Z", "Our posts covered..."). Such a bullet is never a keep for any target:
  cut it. Do not invent an action ("researched the sectors") to keep the topics on the page.
- Counts that do not show impact or scale for this target (e.g. episode counts for a non-media role).
- Anything that repeats information already given elsewhere on the page.
- Typos: a recruiter reads them as carelessness. Judge spelling from the rendered document, not from
  extracted text: PDF text extraction often inserts spaces inside words that look fine on the page.
A signed offer listed as "Incoming <role>, <start date>" is a strong signal and stays.
- Skills listed without evidence anywhere in the CV.
Education for students: university, degree, dates, GPA with scale, and at most one line of relevant
coursework or honours. Longer education sections are justified only for research roles.

## Things students usually undersell (ask about them in Grill me)
Case competitions and hackathons (placement out of N), student organisation roles, volunteering,
coursework or thesis projects with real data, teaching or research assistance, tutoring, freelance,
online courses with a finished project, olympiads, scholarships, exchange semesters, events organised
(participants, budget, sponsors), part-time jobs even if unrelated (work experience of any kind helps).

## Role disambiguation
The same title means different work in different places:
- Business Analyst in consulting: structured problem solving, Excel/PowerPoint, market research, client
  communication. In fintech or product IT: requirements, user stories, SQL, BPMN, working with engineers.
- Data Analyst in a bank: SQL, reporting, risk data, accuracy. In a product company: SQL, A/B tests,
  product metrics, dashboards, Python.
- Project Manager in IT outsourcing: delivery, Jira, Scrum, client communication. In NGO or public
  sector: grants, donors, reporting, stakeholder coordination.
Tie advice to the company type and the vacancy text. When neither is given, state your assumption.

## Strong action verbs
Leadership: Led, Founded, Launched, Coordinated, Organized, Mentored, Initiated.
Analysis: Analyzed, Modeled, Estimated, Forecasted, Investigated, Surveyed, Evaluated, Tested, Validated.
Building: Built, Developed, Designed, Implemented, Automated, Deployed, Optimized, Integrated.
Communication: Presented, Authored, Drafted, Negotiated, Pitched, Facilitated, Translated.
Finance: Budgeted, Valued, Reconciled, Audited, Projected. Results: Increased, Reduced, Streamlined, Won.

## Example bullets
Examples in the program rubrics show the target style only. Never copy their facts, numbers or tools
into a candidate's CV.

## Gaps advice
Recommend what moves the needle for this exact target: specific tools, a portfolio project with a clear
deliverable and link, a language certificate with a target score, a relevant certification, a case
competition. Prefer free or cheap options doable during the semester. Order by impact per week of effort.
